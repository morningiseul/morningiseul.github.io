## Chapter 1. NO GRIP

[Oh... what a shame. Has Zephyr once again misjudged their downforce setup? That cornering is really holding them back!]

On the tablet screen, a blue car wobbled precariously as it took the corner. Moments later, the team radio crackled to life, relaying a man’s voice. It was thick with exertion, each word wrenched out against the crushing force of the G-load.

*&ndash; Car feels off. The brakes are responding too slowly.*

*&ndash; Adrian, box now.*

*&ndash; Are you kidding me? I’m right on his tail, and you want me to box?*

*&ndash; Call’s been made for tires.*

*&ndash; I already told you I can keep going! What do you mean, tires? These softs are good for another five laps!*

*&ndash; Follow instructions.*

*&ndash; For &#42;&#42;&#42;&#42;’s sake! If I don’t pass him now, I never will! Do you not understand that?*

Per F1 broadcasting regulations, the driver’s profanity was dutifully bleeped out. However, his raw, unfiltered frustration bled through all the same.

[And Adrian Falken surrenders eighth place to Harrington’s Tyler McConnell!]

[If only he had a touch more stability through the corners, he might have held onto eighth and perhaps even mounted a challenge for seventh on the ensuing straight.]

[Absolutely! Had he just maintained his pace, he could have lined up a counterattack in the next DRS zone. Such a pity. He’s already fallen over two seconds adrift of McConnell. And to make matters worse, it looks like he’s going in for a tire change. McConnell, meanwhile, is running on hards, which means he’s got tire life to spare, doesn’t he? At this rate, even if Falken pits, by the time he gets any real temperature into those new tires... Huuuh?]

*Crash!*

As the blue car reached the sharp hairpin turn, it slammed into the barricade, thick white smoke billowing from its tail.

[Adrian Falken! He’s lost control of the car and slammed into the wall! It doesn’t seem like he’ll be able to get going again... Indeed, he’s retiring from the race. Double yellow flags are out!]

Track marshals brandished yellow flags from behind the relative safety of the barriers. The chasing pack, which had been sweeping through the corner at roughly 100 km/h, slowed in unison. The driver hauled himself free of the smouldering cockpit, his helmet and race suit bristling with sponsor logos.

He tore off his helmet and peeled away his fireproof balaclava, exposing a face slick with sweat. Droplets scattered into the air as he shook his blond hair, disgruntled.

[Wow... so handsome.]

[Shame about his race results, though.]

The live feed captured every last drop of the driver’s unfiltered vexation. The man took a swig from his bottle of water, his lips distinctly mouthing a string of expletives.

[How did our super rookie end up like this?]

[It’s long been established that F2 success doesn’t automatically translate to F1.]

[True, it’s hardly a unique tale, but… he had the whole world watching him just two years ago.]

[What’s the matter, Emily? Adrian Falken is living proof that teams need to exercise caution. Rush a call-up based purely on F2 results, and this is what you get.]

[It honestly breaks my heart. This entire season is essentially just one prolonged audition for him.]

[His contract expires this year, doesn’t it? If things carry on like this, he’ll be out of a seat. Just two years after his debut.]

Cha Yihyun, the man who’d been observing the screen, dragged the progress bar back to an earlier point in the race.

[They’ve been given the all-clear for DRS. Falken opens the rear wing! They’re going wheel-to-wheel! Falken goes for the overtake on the outside!]

[It’s a fierce battle! Tyler McConnell holds him off... Wait, he made the pass! He’s past him! Zephyr’s Adrian Falken overpowers Harrington’s Tyler McConnell to snatch eighth place!]

[What’s come over Falken today? Has he finally found his wings? He started all the way down in seventeenth and now he’s running eighth. With momentum like this, he might just be eyeing a maiden podium! And we haven’t even reached the twenty-lap mark!]

[This is already a career best for him! His highest finish before today was fifteenth.]

[Has Zephyr finally managed to improve that car of theirs?]

“&#46;&#46;&#46;&#46;&#46;&#46;”

They were seriously debating whether to sign this guy?

To be fair, he did possess a striking face. He’d probably be great for PR campaigns and self-produced YouTube content.

But at the end of the day, what use was an F1 driver who had failed to prove himself after two full seasons? The team would simply become a laughingstock for willingly taking on a ticking time bomb.

It was true that once a driver reached a certain level of proficiency, the machinery beneath him became paramount. But Zephyr was hardly operating on a shoestring budget like Nordhaven Racing. Even if Zephyr’s lead driver wasn’t challenging for podiums, he was still reliably finishing between eighth and tenth. Meanwhile, their German driver, Adrian Falken, was somehow always crossing the finish line behind Nordhaven’s first driver.

It was hardly an uncommon tale: a super rookie promoted to the big leagues, only to find himself relegated to the second car of a lower-tier team. But even making allowances for their shoestring budget and garbage machinery, nothing about his driving particularly screamed exceptional. Not that he was a poor driver by any means, but...

Perhaps he was just missing something because he’d never had cause to look deeper.

Then again, didn’t two years of results and the relentless criticism surrounding him speak for themselves?

Glory in F2 was just that—glory in F2.

It didn’t matter how incredible a driver used to be, or how much god-given talent they supposedly had. A driver who couldn’t prove themselves on the track was nothing more than a loser.

As one star dims, another rises to take its place. If a sensational super rookie failed to live up to expectations and faded into obscurity, his vacant seat would simply become an opportunity for the next rookie in line.

Frankly, Cha Yihyun couldn’t have cared less who the team signed or let go. Whether a floundering prospect, two years removed from his debut, finally lost his seat after failing to prove his worth—or somehow managed to claim the lead driver position for the team he sponsored—none of it mattered to him.

Absurd as it might have sounded, that was simply the reality of the situation. From Cha Yihyun’s standpoint, his sponsorship agreement was nearing its natural conclusion anyway. Should the team continue to underperform, he could simply take his money elsewhere.

The team principal and the other sponsors engaging in pathetic power struggles over next year’s seats was nothing more than a storm in a teacup. He had no patience for their attempts to pull him into their little political games, even if they tried to disguise it as valuing a key sponsor’s input.

Cha Yihyun didn’t own Aquila Dynamics. Between their decidedly middling record on the track, the ceaseless internal scheming, and a team principal whose recent sex scandal had splashed them across every tabloid going, he’d already had more than his fill.

Despite bleeding him for astronomical sums of money, the team had limped to a dismal fifth place out of ten in last year’s Constructors’ Championship. He understood that motorsport was a bottomless money pit, but this was taking the piss. This wasn’t some fledgling outfit still finding its feet in its first or second season; they already possessed the technical expertise and the operational know-how. There was simply no excuse for them to be churning out such bullshit results.

If he’d known things would turn out like this, he never would have signed with them in the first place.

When they had seized second place during his first year of sponsorship, he thought Aquila had finally gotten their act together. He’d expected them to hold steady last season—or, at the very worst, slip to third—but there hadn’t been so much as a flicker of a rebound. Hell, had the atmosphere within the team even been halfway decent, he might have held out hope for next year.

But the fact that this year’s projected Constructors’ standing had plummeted yet another rank to a dismal sixth—honestly, it was just pathetic.

For a team that touted itself as F1’s rising powerhouse, the fact that their second driver was already looking to jump ship was evidence enough that they were past saving.

He wasn’t exactly thrilled at the prospect of having poured two years of his youth down the drain.

“What a fucking joke.”

With more money than he knew what to do with and no desire to take on the tedium of hands-on management, Cha Yihyun had brought his deep pockets to Formula One with a considerably grander scheme in mind.

Formula One might not have drawn enormous numbers back home, but on the global stage, the sport commanded a following of nearly 800 million fans. A single Grand Prix attracted 300,000 live attendees and averaged 2.3 million streamers in the US alone. For a corporation seeking to cement its foothold in the global energy and chemicals market, the investment more than justified itself. By his rough calculations, the brand exposure alone was worth upward of $600 million.

People loved to rail against the sport for ravaging the environment, but those same hypocrites were perfectly content to cruise about on ocean-polluting yachts and enjoy golf and skiing on mountains that had been levelled to the ground. Besides, Cha Yihyun’s family conglomerate, Hystra, wasn’t exactly peddling some purely green eco-business anyway; their focus was sustainable energy. The PR synergy alone made the leap more than worthwhile.

Still, it was his money funding the team. He’d hoped, at the very least, to watch them clinch a championship just once. Instead, all he’d gotten for his trouble was a spike in his blood pressure.

*Do you have any idea what I went through just to get our corporate logo slapped onto those mint-coloured race suits?*

He’d even fretted that corporate funds alone wouldn’t be enough to assert his presence, so he’d sunk his own private fortune into a personal sponsorship for a driver he had no interest in whatsoever. And this was in spite of the fact that he’d already had his eye on a different driver.

Cha Yihyun had set his own personal interests aside and thrown his weight behind this outfit for one reason alone: results.

So why the hell was the car regressing with every passing race?

Another team operating on a similar budget had produced an absolute rocket ship of an engine, making them the runaway favourites for this year’s Constructors’ Championship.

How was it that their mechanics and engineers—people who lived and breathed engineering—had managed to take their early-season problems and exacerbate them halfway through the year? And what on earth was going on with their race strategy? It wasn’t as though they were sourcing their engines from an external supplier, either. No, they were developing their own. For a works team to bungle their own machinery this badly, there was little doubt as to where the blame lay.

Maybe he should’ve just invested in stocks instead. It felt like he was just sitting there, sucking his thumb while watching his shares take a nosedive with each passing day.

With internal sources all but confirming there would be no vehicle upgrades even after the impending summer break, he held little hope for a turnaround in the second half of the season.

The drivers weren’t the issue here. Nor was it the fault of the brilliant pit crew, who, despite a slight dip in form, still drew praise from the fans for consistently pulling off two-second-flat tire changes.

The real culprits were the idiots calling the shots and the leeches constantly chiming in to add their two cents.

As the saying goes, too many cooks spoil the broth. If they were determined to play politics, they could at least have built a good car in the process, but they still hadn’t resolved that shitty coolant issue.

He was in no position to laugh at Adrian Falken’s shitbox of a car.

Right, better to fold a losing hand and just focus on next year’s car. Of course, the odds were they’d simply make a mess of that as well with their petty politicking, but Cha Yihyun wouldn’t be around to witness it.

The clever ones always bailed before the ship went down.

The whole thing was revolting. Their shoddy machinery, their brainless leadership incapable of making even basic strategic calls, and the swarm of parasites clinging to both the first and second drivers, trying to scrape off whatever profit they could.

But it wasn’t as though he could just step in and take control. There were far too many entrenched interests tangled up within the team. Cha Yihyun was already far, far too drained to intervene, and with the title sponsor leading the political circus, there was a hard ceiling on how much weight his opinion could carry anyway. He had no interest in joining a mudslinging competition.

True to a sport where astronomical capital and vested interests collided, everyone was out for blood. In this industry, the mechanics, the crew, and the drivers were the most innocent ones.

Formula One, a sport that set spectators’ hearts ablaze with roaring exhaust notes and blistering speeds, was an entirely different beast behind closed doors.

At the end of the day, people were the same wherever you went. Having witnessed the sport’s ugly underbelly up close, whatever fondness he’d once harboured was rapidly fading away.

Cha Yihyun grabbed his tablet and rose to his feet.

The race car-shaped keyring he’d carelessly stuffed into his pocket jangled softly with the movement.

It was time to catch his flight.
